Ordering product samples from China should answer a business question: **can this supplier reproduce your requirements under controlled conditions?** A sample is useful only when the request, inspection, revisions, and approval are documented well enough to guide the bulk order.

Do not treat the sample as a souvenir or as proof that every future unit will be identical. Use it as one part of a controlled specification and quality process.

## Decide which sample you are ordering

Suppliers may use the word “sample” for very different things. State the sample stage in writing before discussing price.

| Sample type | What it can show | What it cannot prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stock sample | Existing workmanship, general materials, size, and supplier responsiveness | Your custom color, tooling, logo, packaging, or production process |
| Customized sample | Whether the supplier can interpret your design and execute selected changes | Stable mass-production output unless production materials and processes are the same |
| Pre-production sample | The final product configuration before the bulk run | Lot-wide consistency or the absence of production defects |
| Production sample | A unit selected from the actual production lot | Conformance of the rest of the lot without a defined sampling and inspection plan |

If you are still identifying candidates, a stock sample may be enough to remove weak suppliers. If you are about to approve production, require a sample that uses the intended materials, finish, components, artwork, and packaging.

## Prepare one sample brief for every supplier

Before requesting samples, create a short controlled brief. Send the same revision to every candidate so the comparison is meaningful.

Include:

- product name, model, and revision number;
- drawings, dimensions, tolerances, and materials;
- color standard rather than a screen color alone;
- finish, texture, print, logo, and artwork files;
- functional and performance requirements;
- included components, accessories, labels, and instructions;
- retail packaging and master-carton expectations;
- required tests or destination-market constraints already identified;
- sample quantity and delivery address;
- the questions this sample must answer.

If the product definition is still incomplete, say which features are open. Otherwise, a supplier may quietly make a design choice that later appears to be an approved requirement.

For a new supplier, complete basic [supplier verification before paying](/blog/verify-chinese-supplier/) and make sure the quotation, invoice, and payment beneficiary refer to an understood legal entity.

## Request an itemized sample quotation

Ask suppliers to separate the commercial parts of the sample order:

1. sample unit cost;
2. customization or development charge;
3. tooling, mold, printing plate, or setup charge;
4. packaging prototype charge;
5. domestic China transport, if any;
6. international courier or freight;
7. payment fee;
8. sample lead time and dispatch date;
9. whether any charge is credited against a bulk order.

Do not compare only the sample price. A low sample charge can hide expensive tooling, limited revision rounds, or a design that cannot be produced at the target volume.

Record what the supplier will deliver. “One custom sample” is too vague when you need a working unit, printed retail box, accessories, and test evidence.

## Control files, payment, and shipment identity

Give the sample request its own reference number. Put that reference on the brief, supplier invoice, courier record, photographs, inspection notes, and approval decision.

Before paying, confirm:

- the beneficiary and payment method;
- who owns any tooling or paid development output;
- how many revisions are included;
- whether the supplier may substitute a material or process;
- when the supplier must request written approval;
- who pays for a replacement when the sample misses the agreed brief.

Ask the supplier to photograph the completed sample beside its reference label before dispatch. This does not replace your inspection, but it helps prevent sample mix-ups and gives you a record of what left the supplier.

## Inspect the sample against the brief

Do not begin with “Do I like it?” Begin with the controlled requirements.

Use a sample review record with five columns:

| Requirement | Method | Expected result | Actual result | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Material | Document review or test | Approved grade or construction | Evidence and observation | Pass / revise / test |
| Dimensions | Defined measuring equipment | Drawing value and tolerance | Recorded measurements | Pass / fail |
| Appearance | Approved visual standard and lighting | Color, finish, print, workmanship | Photos and notes | Pass / revise |
| Function | Repeatable use or performance test | Defined acceptance criterion | Result and conditions | Pass / fail |
| Packaging | Pack-out review | Correct protection, labels, and contents | Photos and findings | Pass / revise |

Photograph defects with a scale or reference where useful. Separate a preference from a nonconformity: “make it feel more premium” is not actionable, while “increase wall thickness from the current measurement to the specified range” is.

If specialist testing is required, identify the correct laboratory, test method, sample conditioning, and pass criteria before interpreting a result. A supplier's informal statement is not a substitute for required compliance evidence.

## Compare supplier execution, not only the object

The sample also reveals how the supplier manages work. Score each candidate on:

- accuracy of questions before production;
- ability to identify conflicting requirements;
- revision control;
- technical explanation of materials and processes;
- sample lead-time reliability;
- packaging and dispatch discipline;
- openness about subcontracting or limitations;
- quality of corrective action after feedback.

A visually acceptable sample produced through undocumented substitutions may be a weaker choice than a near-acceptable sample from a supplier that records the gap and corrects it systematically.

## Approve with a version and conditions

There are three useful decisions:

- **Approved:** the identified revision may be used as one production reference.
- **Approved with documented conditions:** listed issues must be corrected or monitored without another full sample round.
- **Not approved:** production must not start; the supplier must submit a revised sample or evidence.

Label an approved reference sample with the product code, revision, approval date, approver, and any limitations. When practical, keep one controlled unit and ask the supplier to keep a matching unit with the same reference.

A “golden sample” does not replace written specifications. Color can fade, flexible materials can deform, and an unmeasured physical sample cannot communicate tolerances, tests, or hidden material requirements.

## Transfer the approval into the bulk order

The final [China purchase order checklist](/blog/china-purchase-order-checklist/) should reference the approved sample revision and the full specification pack. It should also define:

- which characteristics are controlled by written values;
- which visual attributes use the approved sample as a reference;
- permitted variation;
- inspection timing and acceptance criteria;
- change-approval rules;
- what happens if production differs from the approved basis.

Do not allow a sample approval to exist only in chat. The people purchasing materials, making the product, packing the order, and performing the [pre-shipment inspection](/blog/pre-shipment-inspection-china/) need the same controlled information.

## Common sample-order mistakes

- Requesting different specifications from different suppliers, then comparing price and quality as if the samples were equivalent.
- Approving a stock sample for a customized production order.
- Using photographs as the only approval evidence.
- Failing to record which sample revision was approved.
- Sending a deposit before commercial and technical documents agree.
- Assuming an excellent hand-made sample proves mass-production capability.
- Approving the product while ignoring packaging, labels, instructions, and accessories.
- Letting sample changes remain in email or chat instead of updating the controlled specification.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I order samples from more than one Chinese supplier?

Usually, yes, when the product and development cost allow it. Parallel samples reduce dependence on one sales presentation and reveal differences in technical interpretation, communication, and process discipline. Use the same brief and review method for each supplier.

### Is a free sample better than a paid sample?

Not necessarily. The important questions are whether the sample represents the intended product, what work is included, and whether the supplier records revisions. Paying a reasonable documented development cost may produce better evidence than accepting an unrelated stock unit.

### Can I approve mass production from photos or video?

Only for attributes that can genuinely be evaluated that way and only at an accepted level of risk. Dimensions, materials, fit, performance, color, and packaging protection often require physical review or testing.

### When should I negotiate the MOQ?

Discuss commercial feasibility early, but finalize the relationship between quantity, customization, unit cost, and setup charges after the product definition is stable. Use the [MOQ and unit-cost guide](/blog/moq-negotiation-unit-cost/) to separate real cost drivers from a headline minimum.
