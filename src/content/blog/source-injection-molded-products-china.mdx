Injection molding is often described as a unit-price problem. In practice, the early decisions are about geometry, resin, tooling, surface requirements, production volume, and who controls the approved part.

The cheapest quote can become the expensive one if a tool needs repeated changes, the resin is substituted, the part warps, or the buyer discovers that the quoted cycle time does not support the required volume.

## Define the part before requesting a mold price

Send a supplier more than a product photograph. A useful brief includes:

- 3D model and controlled drawing revision;
- overall dimensions and critical tolerances;
- wall thickness, ribs, bosses, snaps, threads, and draft assumptions;
- resin grade, color, additives, and approved alternatives;
- surface finish, texture, gloss, or decoration;
- expected load, temperature, chemical, or outdoor exposure;
- assembly partners and mating parts;
- annual demand, first-order quantity, and variant mix;
- destination market and product claims;
- packaging and inspection requirements.

If the design is not ready for manufacture, ask the supplier to list its proposed changes separately from the baseline quote. Do not approve a lower price that quietly changes the part.

## Ask about design-for-manufacturing constraints

The part must leave the mold consistently. Ask the engineering team to identify:

- draft needed for the selected texture and geometry;
- uniform or intentionally controlled wall thickness;
- sink, warp, weld-line, and air-trap risks;
- gate, runner, ejector, and parting-line locations;
- undercuts and side actions;
- insert, overmolding, or secondary-assembly needs;
- shrinkage assumptions for the chosen resin;
- mold-flow or trial analysis where the risk justifies it.

The buyer does not need to accept every supplier preference, but every proposed change should have a reason, affected dimension, and approval owner.

## Choose resin and color as controlled requirements

“ABS,” “PP,” or “food grade” is not a complete material specification. Record the exact grade or performance need where it affects function, safety, appearance, or compliance.

Review:

- base resin and approved grade;
- recycled or regrind content limits;
- flame, UV, impact, temperature, or chemical performance;
- color standard and viewing conditions;
- masterbatch or pigment control;
- surface finish and gloss;
- material lot traceability;
- supplier of resin and change notification.

For regulated or food-contact products, a supplier statement is not the same as destination-market evidence. Use the [product compliance research checklist](/blog/china-product-compliance-checklist/) and qualified laboratories or specialists as needed.

## Compare mold quotations by output and scope

Request a separate line for:

- mold base, steel, cavities, and inserts;
- hot-runner or cold-runner system;
- slides, lifters, unscrewing, or other mechanisms;
- polishing and texture;
- mold-flow or design review;
- trial shots and sample quantities;
- dimensional report;
- included modifications after trial;
- maintenance, repair, and spare parts;
- expected cycle time and output;
- mold storage, identification, and transfer terms;
- part price by quantity tier.

Mold life is not a single universal number. It depends on steel, resin, cycle, maintenance, geometry, and acceptance criteria. Ask what the quoted expectation means and what evidence supports it.

## Define tooling ownership and access operationally

Tooling terms should cover more than the sentence “the buyer owns the mold.” Clarify:

- how the tool is identified;
- where it is stored;
- who may authorize changes;
- who pays for maintenance, repairs, and replacement;
- whether the supplier may use it for another customer;
- how mold condition and shot count are recorded;
- what happens if production moves or the relationship ends;
- how the tool and related data are transferred.

Ownership and intellectual-property rights require appropriate legal advice. Operational records still matter because an unmarked or inaccessible mold is difficult to control in practice.

## Approve trials for both part and process

Ask for a trial plan that identifies machine, resin lot, color, mold revision, process settings recorded by the factory, and sample quantity. The buyer should receive:

- first-shot or trial samples;
- dimensions at agreed locations;
- photographs of appearance and parting line;
- assembly or functional checks;
- weight where it is a process indicator;
- visible defect log;
- proposed changes and their effect;
- sample revision and approval status.

A hand-finished sample can hide a process problem. Approve a sample made with the intended mold, material, machine family, and production method whenever practical.

## Plan quality control around molding risks

The inspection plan should reflect the part, not a generic cosmetic checklist.

Consider:

- first-piece approval at setup;
- resin and color verification;
- critical dimensions and datum method;
- warpage, sink, short shot, flash, burn, weld line, and contamination;
- gate and ejector marks;
- assembly fit and function;
- weight or density trend;
- insert presence and orientation;
- packaging protection;
- lot traceability and retained samples.

Use the [AQL inspection guide](/blog/aql-inspection-china/) only as one part of the sampling decision. Safety, regulatory, material, and critical functional characteristics may need separate controls or testing.

## Treat MOQ and cycle time as linked

Molded parts can have a low unit price at high volume while still carrying significant setup, resin, color, packaging, and machine-change costs. Ask which MOQ comes from:

- mold setup;
- resin or color batch;
- machine allocation;
- packaging print;
- assembly;
- customer-specific variant;
- supplier’s economic production run.

Test a pilot using the intended process, but do not assume a small quantity will have the same unit economics as a stable repeat run. The [small-batch manufacturing guide](/blog/small-batch-manufacturing-china/) explains how to separate these minimums.

## Control changes after tool approval

Require written approval before changing resin, color, supplier, machine, mold location, insert, gate, process window, subcontractor, packaging, or inspection method when the change can affect the part.

The change record should state:

- reason and affected lot;
- old and new material or process;
- dimensional, functional, appearance, and compliance impact;
- sample or test required;
- cost and schedule effect;
- approval owner and new revision.

Urgent production pressure is not a reason to bypass revision control. If a temporary deviation is accepted, record its quantity, expiry, and follow-up.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does injection molding cost in China?

There is no reliable universal price. Cost depends on part geometry, steel, cavities, mechanisms, surface finish, resin, tolerances, trial scope, quantity, and the supplier’s process. Request an itemized mold and part quote on one controlled design.

### How do I choose an injection-molding factory in China?

Check whether the site has relevant machines, engineering capability, tool control, resin and color handling, quality records, assembly or subcontracting control, and a realistic plan for your part. Confirm identity and payment structure before development funds.

### What is a good MOQ for a molded product?

It depends on the material batch, machine setup, packaging, demand, and supplier economics. Ask which layer creates the minimum and model a pilot separately from the repeat-order cost.

### Should the supplier or buyer own the mold?

The answer depends on design, payment, contract, future volume, and the relationship. Document ownership, access, maintenance, identification, relocation, and transfer terms with qualified legal advice.

## Make the first order a process test

Before scaling, compare the approved trial with production measurements, defects, cycle evidence, pack-out, inspection results, and actual lead time. Update the drawing, material record, mold file, cost model, and purchase order after the first lot teaches you something.
