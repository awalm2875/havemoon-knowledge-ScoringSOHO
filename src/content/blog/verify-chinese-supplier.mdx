A deposit creates leverage for the supplier and exposure for the buyer. Verification should therefore be completed **before** money is sent, not after production is already late.

Supplier verification is not one database search or one factory video. It is a chain of matching evidence: company identity, payment beneficiary, operating site, product capability, quality controls, and contract documents should tell the same story.

## Start with the legal Chinese company name

Ask for a clear copy of the current business license. Record:

- the Chinese legal name;
- unified social credit code;
- legal representative;
- registered address;
- date of establishment;
- registered business scope;
- registration status.

Search that identity in China’s official [National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System](https://www.gsxt.gov.cn/index.html). Transliteration is unreliable, so search using the Chinese characters or credit code. A registered company is not automatically a capable factory, but a name mismatch is a reason to stop and investigate.

Compare the legal name with the quotation, contract, invoice, email signature, website footer, and any certificates. If an export company, Hong Kong company, or related trading entity will contract or receive funds, request a written explanation of the relationship and verify both entities.

## Verify the bank beneficiary independently

The account name should normally match the contracting entity. If it does not, require a documented commercial reason and professional advice appropriate to the payment structure.

Before paying:

1. Obtain payment instructions on a formal document.
2. Compare the beneficiary name with the signed contract.
3. Confirm bank details using a known phone number or video call—not by replying to the same email that requested the change.
4. Ask a second person in your organization to review the amount and beneficiary.
5. Save the verified instructions in the order file.

Treat urgency, secrecy, a personal account, cryptocurrency, a sudden country change, or a last-minute beneficiary change as high-risk signals. Email accounts can be compromised even when the supplier is genuine.

## Confirm the operating address and business model

The registered address may be an office rather than the production site. Ask for the factory address in Chinese and English, then compare it with shipping documents, audit reports, map listings, and live visual evidence.

Establish whether the company is:

- the manufacturer operating the site;
- a manufacturer with outsourced processes;
- a trading company coordinating another factory;
- an exporter related to the factory;
- an unrelated intermediary.

None of these models is inherently disqualifying. The risk comes from an undisclosed structure. You need to know who controls materials, production scheduling, inspection, corrective action, intellectual property, and delivery.

## Test product and process knowledge

Ask questions that require manufacturing knowledge rather than sales vocabulary:

- Which process creates the critical dimension or finish?
- What tolerances can the normal production process hold?
- Which defects are most common and where are they detected?
- Which components are purchased and how are incoming lots controlled?
- What is the actual bottleneck at the requested order volume?
- How is traceability maintained from finished goods to material or component lots?
- What happens when an inspection fails?

Compare the answers from sales, engineering, and quality staff. Consistent, specific answers are stronger evidence than a polished company presentation.

## Review quality-control evidence

Request recent examples with sensitive customer information removed:

- incoming inspection records;
- in-process and final inspection forms;
- equipment calibration status;
- nonconformance reports;
- corrective-action records;
- material or batch traceability;
- approved-sample controls;
- test reports relevant to the proposed product.

Check whether the forms are actually used. Blank templates prove very little. Dates, product references, measurements, dispositions, and signatures should be internally consistent.

If a supplier cites ISO 9001, confirm the certificate holder, address, scope, issue date, expiry, and certification body. A quality-management certificate describes a system scope; it does not certify every product or guarantee your order will conform.

## Use a risk-based factory audit

A live video tour can confirm basic facts but is easy to stage. For a material first order, custom tooling, safety-sensitive product, or large deposit, consider an independent on-site audit.

A useful audit covers:

- legal and site identity;
- organization and staffing;
- relevant equipment and maintenance;
- production flow and capacity assumptions;
- purchasing and incoming control;
- process control and work instructions;
- inspection and testing;
- calibration;
- nonconforming-product segregation;
- warehouse and shipment control;
- social, environmental, or security requirements relevant to your program.

Ask the auditor to verify your product’s specific processes. A generic checklist can miss the one machine, test, or subcontractor that controls the main risk.

## Check the commercial documents as one system

Before the deposit, the final quotation, purchase order, contract, specification, artwork, approved sample record, and payment request should agree on:

- product revision and materials;
- quantity and acceptable quantity tolerance;
- unit price and currency;
- tooling and ownership;
- sample and production approvals;
- packaging and labeling;
- inspection method and acceptance criteria;
- production and shipment dates;
- Incoterms rule and named place;
- payment milestones;
- change approval;
- remedies and dispute provisions.

Resolve discrepancies in writing. A sales-chat promise that never reaches the purchase order is difficult to enforce operationally.

## Score evidence and unresolved risks

Use a simple decision log rather than relying on overall impressions.

| Area | Green evidence | Stop or escalate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | License and official record match | Legal name withheld or inconsistent |
| Payment | Beneficiary matches verified contract | Personal or unexplained third-party account |
| Capability | Relevant equipment, records, and technical answers | Only showroom evidence or vague claims |
| Quality | Completed records and traceability | Blank forms or no failure process |
| Contract | One consistent controlled package | Material terms scattered across chats |

Record each unresolved point, owner, due date, and the evidence needed to close it. If a material identity or payment concern remains open, do not send the deposit simply to preserve a quoted price or production slot.

## Verification is not a one-time event

Recheck high-risk information when the legal entity, beneficiary, factory location, tooling location, key staff, or product scope changes. For long-running programs, monitor delivery, inspection, claims, and corrective actions as part of supplier performance.

Good verification does not promise that a supplier will never fail. It ensures that the buyer knows who is being paid, what capability was demonstrated, which risks remain, and what controls are in place before money leaves the account.
