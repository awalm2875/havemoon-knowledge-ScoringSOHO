A purchase order to a Chinese supplier should do more than repeat a quotation. It should connect the commercial agreement to a controlled product definition, quality plan, production schedule, packaging specification, inspection decision, and shipping handoff.

The most useful question is not “Do I have a PO?” It is: **could the supplier's production, quality, packing, and finance teams execute the same order from this document pack without relying on chat history?**

## Build a purchase-order pack, not one overloaded page

The purchase order can be the commercial control document while referenced attachments carry technical detail.

A practical pack may include:

1. the purchase order and line items;
2. product specification or drawing set;
3. bill of materials or approved component list;
4. artwork and packaging files;
5. approved-sample record;
6. inspection specification and acceptance criteria;
7. production milestone plan;
8. applicable contract terms or quality agreement.

Give every controlled file a name, revision, and date. List those revisions on the PO. “See latest artwork” invites two parties to use different files.

## Identify the buyer, supplier, and payment entity

Record the complete legal names and addresses of the contracting parties. For the supplier, use the verified legal entity rather than only an English trading name.

The PO should show:

- buyer legal name, billing address, and contact;
- supplier legal name and registered or agreed operating address;
- purchase-order number and issue date;
- quotation or framework-agreement reference;
- currency and tax treatment where applicable;
- payment beneficiary or a controlled reference to verified payment instructions;
- ship-to, notify, and invoice contacts as needed.

If the factory, exporter, contracting company, and bank beneficiary are different entities, resolve and document the relationship before paying. The [supplier verification workflow](/blog/verify-chinese-supplier/) explains the identity and beneficiary checks that should happen before a deposit.

## Control every line item

Each line should identify exactly what is being purchased. Avoid using only a marketplace listing title or supplier model number.

Include:

- buyer product code and supplier code;
- product name and controlled revision;
- quantity and any permitted quantity tolerance;
- unit of measure;
- unit price and line total;
- tooling, setup, sampling, testing, or packaging charges as separate lines;
- included accessories, spares, labels, and manuals;
- approved substitutions, if any.

When an order contains variants, state which specifications are shared and which differ. A color, plug, language, label, or packaging change can affect materials, testing, MOQ, lead time, and unit cost.

## Reference measurable product specifications

The PO should point to the current specification pack rather than reducing the product to adjectives such as “high quality.”

Depending on the product, control:

- material grade, composition, finish, and approved source where critical;
- dimensions and tolerances;
- color standard and viewing conditions;
- function, capacity, performance, or reliability requirements;
- component brands or approved equivalents;
- workmanship limits and visual defect standards;
- regulatory markings, warnings, labels, and documentation;
- packaging configuration and carton limits;
- traceability, batch, serial, or date coding.

If an [approved product sample](/blog/order-product-samples-from-china/) is part of the standard, reference its sample ID and explain which visual attributes it controls. Keep numeric requirements and test criteria in writing.

## Define quality and acceptance before production

An inspection clause is incomplete unless the supplier and buyer know what will be checked and what result is acceptable.

Record:

- inspection stages: incoming, in-process, pre-shipment, or loading;
- responsible party and access arrangements;
- sampling method where used;
- critical, major, and minor defect definitions where relevant;
- measurement and test methods;
- pass/fail criteria;
- required records and certificates;
- segregation, rework, replacement, and reinspection process;
- shipment-release authority.

Do not write that an inspection “guarantees quality.” Inspection supplies evidence from a defined scope. Production control, supplier capability, specifications, and corrective action still matter.

For the final order stage, align the PO with the [pre-shipment inspection plan](/blog/pre-shipment-inspection-china/) and make sure the supplier understands what evidence is required before release.

## Freeze changes and define approval authority

State that the supplier may not change controlled materials, components, dimensions, processes, subcontractors, tooling location, artwork, packaging, or production site without written approval when those items affect the order.

A useful change request identifies:

- requested change and reason;
- affected product and revision;
- effect on function, appearance, compliance, quality, price, and schedule;
- validation or sample required;
- buyer decision and date;
- new controlled revision after approval.

Verbal urgency should not bypass revision control. If a change is accepted during production, update the documents that inspectors and packing teams will use.

## Turn the quoted lead time into milestones

“30 days after deposit” is not a production plan. Define what starts the clock and which approvals can stop it.

Useful milestones include:

- PO acknowledged;
- deposit received;
- specification and artwork frozen;
- materials ordered and received;
- tooling or first production setup completed;
- pilot or first-off unit approved;
- mass production started and completed;
- inspection-ready date;
- rework allowance;
- packaging completed;
- cargo-ready date;
- shipment booking cutoff.

The [China manufacturing lead-time guide](/blog/china-manufacturing-lead-time/) shows how to build a schedule around evidence and dependencies rather than one promised completion date.

## Specify the delivery rule and named place

Use an Incoterms rule only with its named place or port and the agreed edition, for example “FCA [named place], Incoterms 2020.” The rule allocates defined delivery tasks, costs, and risks; it does not replace product terms, payment conditions, title provisions, or a full transport scope.

Review the [FOB, EXW, and CIF buyer guide](/blog/fob-exw-cif-china-imports/) and the official [ICC Incoterms rules overview](https://iccwbo.org/business-solutions/incoterms-rules/) before using a shorthand that neither party has operationally mapped.

Also record:

- cargo-ready address and contact;
- required booking notice;
- shipping marks and document deadline;
- packaging and pallet requirements;
- party providing commercial invoice, packing list, certificates, and transport data;
- split-shipment or transshipment restrictions where applicable.

## Link payment to evidence and control

Payment milestones should match the risk and evidence available. Record the amount or percentage, due event, currency, beneficiary control, and documents required.

Examples of evidence-based events include:

- signed PO acknowledgement;
- approved final sample or specification;
- tooling acceptance;
- production milestone evidence;
- passed inspection and closed corrective actions;
- complete shipment-document pack.

Do not make a final payment condition impossible to use. If the inspection can occur only after all funds are sent, it cannot serve as a payment gate.

## Require written acknowledgement

Ask the supplier to acknowledge the complete PO pack, not merely reply “received.” The acknowledgement should confirm:

- legal entity and order reference;
- product and attachment revisions;
- quantity, price, and currency;
- payment schedule;
- milestones and cargo-ready date;
- delivery rule and named place;
- open deviations or exceptions;
- authorized supplier approver.

Resolve exceptions before the deposit. Silence should not be treated as acceptance of a detailed attachment.

## Final China purchase order checklist

Before release, verify that the pack answers these questions:

- Who is contracting, manufacturing, exporting, and receiving payment?
- What exact revision, quantity, and configuration is being purchased?
- Which file controls every material product and packaging requirement?
- What may the supplier change, and who can approve it?
- How will conformance be measured and accepted?
- What evidence releases each payment?
- What event starts the production lead time?
- What are the production, inspection, and cargo-ready milestones?
- Which delivery rule, named place, and edition apply?
- Which documents must be provided before shipment?
- What happens after a failed inspection, late order, shortage, or unauthorized change?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a supplier quotation enough without a purchase order?

A quotation may describe price and a broad scope, but it often lacks controlled specifications, acceptance criteria, change rules, milestones, and shipment requirements. A PO pack converts the accepted offer into an operational order record.

### Should the purchase order be bilingual?

Use language that the authorized parties and operating teams can reliably understand. A bilingual document can reduce ambiguity, but translation quality and a clear governing-language clause require professional legal review where contractual effect matters.

### Can the PO replace a manufacturing contract?

That depends on the transaction, governing law, incorporated terms, and how the documents are accepted. Use qualified counsel for contract structure, dispute terms, intellectual property, tooling ownership, and remedies. This guide focuses on operational completeness, not legal sufficiency.

### When should the PO be updated?

Update it or its controlled attachments after any approved change affecting product, quantity, price, schedule, payment, delivery, inspection, packaging, or compliance. Preserve the prior revision and acknowledgement trail.
