Landed cost is the total cost required to bring imported goods to a defined point, ready for the next business step. A useful model goes beyond the supplier’s unit price and international freight. It includes origin charges, insurance, destination fees, customs duty, tax treatment, brokerage, inland delivery, financing, and expected quality loss.

The first rule is to name the endpoint. “Landed” might mean the destination port, the buyer’s warehouse, or a fulfillment center. Two calculations cannot be compared unless they end at the same place and use the same tax assumptions.

## Basic formula

For an order, use:

`Landed order cost = product + origin + freight + insurance + destination + duty + nonrecoverable tax + brokerage + inland + other risk costs`

Then:

`Landed cost per usable unit = landed order cost ÷ expected usable units`

Expected usable units are ordered units minus samples, destructive-test units, expected defects, and losses that cannot be recovered or sold.

## 1. Product and supplier-side costs

Begin with the commercial invoice value, then add costs that may sit outside the quoted unit price:

- product units;
- tooling, setup, or development allocated to the order;
- retail and export packaging;
- labels, manuals, and inserts;
- testing and inspection;
- bank or payment fees;
- expected overrun quantity;
- molds or sample freight if allocated.

Identify the Incoterms rule and named place. An EXW price may exclude loading, pickup, and export handling that an FOB quote includes. A CIF quote includes specified freight and insurance to a named port but may exclude significant destination charges.

## 2. Origin logistics

Depending on the commercial term and shipment, origin costs can include:

- factory pickup;
- consolidation or warehouse handling;
- export customs declaration;
- documentation;
- terminal handling;
- container loading or less-than-container-load charges;
- security, fuel, peak-season, or other carrier surcharges;
- storage or waiting time caused by missed cut-off.

Ask the forwarder for an itemized origin schedule. Clarify which party receives each invoice.

## 3. Main freight and cargo insurance

Use the transport mode and chargeable basis. Air freight often uses the greater of actual and volumetric weight. Ocean less-than-container-load freight may be based on volume with minimum charges, while a full container has equipment, route, and seasonal factors.

Include expected surcharges and realistic exchange rates. For cargo insurance, use the actual premium and coverage. Do not assume a supplier-arranged policy covers the full commercial exposure.

## 4. Customs value and classification

Customs duty depends on destination-country rules, tariff classification, origin, customs value, and any preferential, trade-remedy, or product-specific measures.

In the United States, U.S. Customs and Border Protection publishes [Importing into the United States](https://www.cbp.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Importing%20into%20the%20U.S.pdf), an official overview of entry, classification, valuation, marking, and importer responsibilities. The importer remains responsible for reasonable care even when using a customs broker.

Do not estimate duty from a product keyword alone. Confirm the tariff code and applicable rate with authoritative tariff resources or a qualified customs professional. Consider antidumping, countervailing, safeguard, or special duties where relevant.

## 5. Duty calculation

An ad valorem duty is commonly modeled as:

`Customs value × duty rate`

But the customs-value basis and additions vary by jurisdiction. Assists, royalties, packing, commissions, freight, insurance, and related-party pricing can affect valuation depending on the rules.

Some tariffs use specific rates per unit, weight, volume, or a compound formula. Keep the duty calculation in a separate worksheet with its code, source, rate date, and assumptions.

## 6. Import tax and recovery

VAT, GST, sales tax, or similar charges may be payable at import. Whether they are a recoverable input tax or a permanent cost depends on registration, documentation, product use, and local rules.

Show recoverable tax as a cash-flow requirement rather than silently including or excluding it. If tax cannot be recovered, include it in landed cost. Obtain jurisdiction-specific tax advice.

## 7. Destination charges and final delivery

Destination costs may include:

- terminal or port handling;
- document and carrier fees;
- customs broker and bond;
- government examination or inspection;
- storage, demurrage, and detention;
- deconsolidation;
- port-to-warehouse transport;
- appointment, liftgate, residential, or fulfillment-center charges;
- pallet exchange or rework.

Request a destination charge schedule before selecting a freight offer. A low prepaid ocean rate can be paired with high charges payable on arrival.

## 8. Quality, delay, and financing costs

For commercial decisions, include expected costs even when they do not appear on customs documents:

- inspection and reinspection;
- expected defect, damage, and shortage allowance;
- sorting, rework, disposal, or returns;
- air freight caused by production delay;
- inventory financing during production and transit;
- currency conversion and rate buffer;
- warehouse receiving and labeling;
- obsolescence or markdown allowance.

Keep “customs landed value” and “management landed cost” separate if your finance system uses different definitions.

## Worked example

Suppose a buyer orders 2,000 units:

| Cost item | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Product and packaging | $12,000 |
| Origin and export charges | $850 |
| Freight and insurance | $2,200 |
| Destination and brokerage | $1,050 |
| Duty | $1,080 |
| Nonrecoverable tax/fees | $320 |
| Inland delivery | $500 |
| Inspection and expected loss | $400 |
| **Total** | **$18,400** |

If 1,960 units are expected to be usable, the modeled landed cost is `$18,400 ÷ 1,960 = $9.39` per usable unit. Dividing by the ordered 2,000 units would understate the unit economics.

This example is illustrative only; it is not a tariff or tax determination.

## Use scenarios, not one perfect number

Create at least three cases:

- **Base:** current quotes and expected yield;
- **Low:** favorable freight, exchange rate, and full yield;
- **High:** rate increase, examination, delay, and higher defect allowance.

Track which variables most affect margin. For heavy low-value goods, freight may dominate. For regulated or trade-remedy products, duty and compliance can dominate. For custom goods, defects and tooling may matter more than the supplier’s quoted unit-price difference.

## Landed-cost checklist

- [ ] Endpoint and currency are defined.
- [ ] Incoterms rule, named place, and version are recorded.
- [ ] Product, setup, packaging, and inspection costs are included.
- [ ] Origin, freight, insurance, and destination charges are itemized.
- [ ] Tariff classification, customs value, origin, and duty assumptions are sourced.
- [ ] Recoverable and nonrecoverable tax are separated.
- [ ] Inland delivery and receiving costs are included.
- [ ] Usable-unit yield, defects, delay, and financing are modeled.
- [ ] Low, base, and high scenarios are compared.

The most useful landed-cost model is auditable. Every number should show its source, date, currency, tax treatment, and owner. Update it when the specification, supplier, route, quantity, tariff, or Incoterms rule changes.
